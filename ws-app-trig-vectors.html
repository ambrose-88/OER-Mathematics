    Worksheet D.16 Vectors in Two Dimensions
    

    Definition D.16.500. Geometric Description of Vectors.

    • A vector \(\mathbf{v}\) is a line directed line segment from a point \(P\) to a (possibly non-distinct) point \(Q\text{.}\)
      • \(P\) is called the initial point of the vector \(\mathbf{v}\text{.}\)
        🔗
      • \(Q\) is called the terminal point of the vector \(\mathbf{v}\text{.}\)
        🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    • The magnitude of a vector, denoted \(\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert\text{,}\) is the length of the line segment.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Two vectors are equal if they point in the same direction and have the same magnitude.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    In order to go from the initial point of a vector to its terminal point, you will have to travel a horizontal distance of \(a_1\) and a vertical distance of \(a_2\text{,}\) as illustrated in the image below:
    🔗

    Definition D.16.502. Analytic Definition of Vectors.

    We represent the vector \(\mathbf{v}\) as an ordered pair of numbers as follows
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v}=\langle a_1,a_2\rangle \end{equation*}
    This is known as the component form of the vector \(\mathbf{v}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem D.16.503.

    If \(P=(x_1,y_1)\) and \(Q=(x_2,y_2)\) are the initial and terminal points of a vector \(\mathbf{v}\text{,}\) then the vector is given by
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v}=\langle x_2-x_1, y_2-y_1\rangle \end{equation*}
    This further implies that
    \begin{align*} a_1\amp=x_2-x_1\\ \\ a_2\amp=y_2-y_1 \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Note D.16.504.

    Let \(\mathbf{v}=\langle a_1,a_2\rangle\text{.}\)
    • If \(a_1\neq 0\text{,}\) then the slope of the line segment from the initial point to the terminal point is given by \(\dfrac{a_2}{a_1}\text{.}\) But be careful, the signs of \(a_1\) and \(a_2\) will determine the direction in which the vector points!
      🔗
      🔗
    • If \(a_1=0\) and \(a_2\gt 0\text{,}\) then the line segment is vertical and points straight up.
      🔗
      🔗
    • If \(a_1=0\) and \(a_2\lt 0\text{,}\) then the line segment is vertical and points straight down.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example D.16.505.

    Find the vector \(\mathbf{v}\) with initial point \((-3,2)\) and terminal points \((-1,-4)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem D.16.506. Magnitude of a Vector.

    The magnitude of a vector is given by the formula
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert=\sqrt{a_1^2+a_2^2} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition D.16.507. Unit Vector.

    Any vector \(\mathbf{v}\) that has magnitude 1 is called a unit vector.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example D.16.508. Unit Vectors \(\mathbf{i}\) and \(\mathbf{j}\).

    The two most important unit vectors are
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{i}=\langle 1,0\rangle\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbf{j}=\langle 0,1\rangle \end{equation*}
    🔗
    since they point in the directions of the positive \(x\)- and \(y\)-axes:
    🔗
    Represent the vector \(\mathbf{v}=\langle 3,2\rangle\) in terms of \(\mathbf{i}\) and \(\mathbf{j}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem D.16.511.

    Any vector \(\mathbf{v}=\langle a_1,a_2\rangle\) can be represented in terms of the unit vectors \(\mathbf{i}\) and \(\mathbf{j}\) as
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v}=a_1\mathbf{i}+a_2\mathbf{j} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example D.16.512.

    Let \(P=(-3,4)\) and \(Q=(5,-6)\) be the initial and terminal points of a vector \(\mathbf{v}\) from \(P\) to \(Q\text{.}\) Express the vector \(\mathbf{v}\) using the analytic definition and in terms of the unit vectors \(\mathbf{i}\) and \(\mathbf{j}\text{.}\) Then, find the magnitude of \(\mathbf{v}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition D.16.513. Vector Addition.

    Let \(\mathbf{v}=\langle a_1,a_2\rangle\) and \(\mathbf{w}=\langle b_1,b_2\rangle\) be any two vectors. We define vector addition as
    \begin{align*} \mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w}\amp=\langle a_1+b_1,a_2+b_2\rangle\amp\text{Component Notation}\\ \\ \mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w}\amp=(a_1+b_1)\mathbf{i}+(a_2+b_2)\mathbf{j}\amp\text{Unit Vector Notation} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example D.16.515.

    Find \(\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w}\) when \(\mathbf{v}=3\mathbf{i}-5\mathbf{j}\) and \(\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{i}-3\mathbf{j}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition D.16.516. Vector Subtraction.

    Let \(\mathbf{v}=\langle a_1,a_2\rangle\) and \(\mathbf{w}=\langle b_1,b_2\rangle\) be any two vectors. We define vector subtraction as
    \begin{align*} \mathbf{v}-\mathbf{w}\amp=\langle a_1-b_1,a_2-b_2\rangle\amp\text{Component Notation}\\ \\ \mathbf{v}-\mathbf{w}\amp=(a_1-b_1)\mathbf{i}+(a_2-b_2)\mathbf{j}\amp\text{Unit Vector Notation} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example D.16.518.

    Find \(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{w}\) when \(\mathbf{v}=3\mathbf{i}-5\mathbf{j}\) and \(\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{i}-3\mathbf{j}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition D.16.519. Scalar Multiplication.

    Let \(\mathbf{u}=\langle a_1,a_2\rangle\) be any vector and let \(\lambda\) be any real number. We define scalar multiplication as
    \begin{align*} \lambda\mathbf{v}\amp=\langle \lambda a_1,\lambda a_2\rangle\amp\text{Component Notation}\\ \\ \lambda\mathbf{v}\amp=(\lambda a_1)\mathbf{i}+(\lambda a_2)\mathbf{j}\amp\text{Unit Vector Notation} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Note D.16.520.

    There is another type of multiplication called vector multiplication. It is more complicated and will not be discussed in this course.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example D.16.522.

    Find \(\lambda\mathbf{v}\) when \(\mathbf{v}=3\mathbf{i}-5\mathbf{j}\) and \(\lambda=4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition D.16.523. Zero Vector.

    We defined the zero vector as
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \mathbf{0}\amp=\langle 0, 0\rangle\amp\text{Component Notation}\\ \\ \mathbf{0}\amp=0\mathbf{i}+0\mathbf{j}\amp\text{Unit Vector Notation} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem D.16.524. Properties of Vectors.

    The following properties (known as the field axioms) are given in three broad categories
    🔗
    • Vector Addition:
      • Vector Addition is Commutative: \(\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{w}+\mathbf{v}\text{.}\)
        🔗
        🔗
      • Vector Addition is Associative: \((\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v})+\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{u}+(\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w})\)
        🔗
        🔗
      • Anything plus the Zero Vector is itself: \(\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{0}=\mathbf{v}\)
        🔗
        🔗
      • Additive Inverses Exist: \(\mathbf{v}+(-\mathbf{v})=\mathbf{0}\)
        🔗
        🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    • Length of a vector:
      • Scalars increase/decrease the magnitude of a vector: \(|\lambda\mathbf{v}|=|\lambda|\cdot|\mathbf{v}|\)
        🔗
        🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    • Multiplication by a Scalar:
      • Scalar Distribution Property: \(\lambda(\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w})=\lambda\mathbf{v}+\lambda\mathbf{w}\)
        🔗
        🔗
      • Vector Distribution Property: \((\lambda_1+\lambda_2)\mathbf{v}=\lambda_1\mathbf{v}+\lambda_2\mathbf{v}\)
        🔗
        🔗
      • Scalar Multiplication is Commutative: \(\lambda_1(\lambda_2\mathbf{v})=\lambda_2(\lambda_1\mathbf{v})\)
        🔗
        🔗
      • Scalar Multiplication is Associative: \((\lambda_1\lambda_2)\mathbf{v}=\lambda_1(\lambda_2\mathbf{v})\)
        🔗
        🔗
      • Any vector times the scalar 1 is itself: \(1\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}\)
        🔗
        🔗
      • Any vector times the scalar 0 is the zero vector: \(0\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}\)
        🔗
        🔗
      • Any scalar times the zero vector is the zero vector: \(\lambda\mathbf{0}=\mathbf{0}\)
        🔗
        🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example D.16.525.

    Let \(\mathbf{v}=2\mathbf{i}-3\mathbf{j}\) and \(\mathbf{w}=-5\mathbf{i}+\mathbf{j}\text{.}\) Compute \(-2\mathbf{v}+3\mathbf{w}\) using component notation and unit vector notation.
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem D.16.526.

    For any nonzero vector \(\mathbf{v}\text{,}\) the unit vector in the direction of \(\mathbf{v}\) is given by
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{1}{\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert}\mathbf{v}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example D.16.527.

    Find the unit vector in the direction of \(\mathbf{v}=2\mathbf{i}-3\mathbf{j}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem D.16.528. Horizontal and Vertical Components of a Vector.

    Let \(\mathbf{v}\) be a vector with magnitude \(\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert\) and direction \(\theta\text{.}\) Then the vector can be computed as
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v}=\langle\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert\cos(\theta),\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert\sin(\theta)\rangle \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Written in terms of \(\mathbf{i}\) and \(\mathbf{j}\text{,}\) the vector can be written as
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v}=\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert\cos(\theta)\mathbf{i}+\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert\sin(\theta)\mathbf{j} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example D.16.529.

    A vector has magnitude 12 and direction \(\dfrac{\pi}{4}\text{.}\) Find the horizontal and vertical components, and use them to write the vector in terms of \(\mathbf{i}\) and \(\mathbf{j}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition D.16.530. Velocity and Speed.

    • The velocity of an object in motion is given by the vector \(\mathbf{v}\text{.}\)
      🔗
    • The speed of an object in motion traveling with velocity \(\mathbf{v}\) is given by \(\left|\mathbf{v}\right|\text{.}\)
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example D.16.531. Airplane with Crosswind.

    🔗
    Express the velocity of the airplane relative to the air in component form.
    🔗
    Express the velocity of the wind in component form.
    🔗
    Find the true velocity of the plane.
    🔗
    Find the true speed of the plane.
    🔗
