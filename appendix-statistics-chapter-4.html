    Definition H.4.812. What are Sample Spaces and Events?

    The sample space of an experiment is the population of all unique possible results that could occur in that experiment. It is typically denoted by \(S\text{.}\)
    🔗
    An outcome is a single possible result of an experiment. Similar to data, we often use \(x\) to denote an outcome.
    🔗
    🔗

    An event is a subset of a sample space that satisfies a given condition.

    Definition H.4.820. The Number of Outcomes.

    The notation \(n(A)\) represents the number of elements in a set \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    • For a sample space \(S\text{,}\) \(n(S)\) gives the total number of possible outcomes in the experiment.
      🔗
    • For an event \(E\text{,}\) \(n(E)\) represents the number of outcomes that satisfy the event condition.
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition H.4.824. What is Theoretical Probability?

    The theoretical probability of an event \(E\) is given by:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} P(E)=\dfrac{n(E)}{n(S)} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    • \(n(E)\) is the number of outcomes in the event.
      🔗
    • \(n(S)\) is the total number of outcomes in the sample space.
      🔗
    🔗
    Theoretical probability assumes that all outcomes in the sample space are equally likely.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition H.4.834. What is Empirical Probability?

    For a sample of size \(n\text{,}\) the empirical probability that a randomly chosen individual has property \(E\) is:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} P(E)=\dfrac{n(E)}{n}=\dfrac{\text{number with property }E}{\text{sample size}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This is the same formula as relative frequency in a sample.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition H.4.837. What is the Difference Between Probability and Relative Frequency?

    • Relative frequency tells us what percentage of observed data has a given property.
      🔗
    • Empirical probability estimates the chance a randomly selected individual from a sample has a given property.
      🔗
    • Theoretical probability is the exact long-run proportion when an experiment is repeated indefinitely under equal-likelihood assumptions.
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition H.4.841. What are the Properties of Probability?

    The empty set, denoted \(\emptyset\text{,}\) contains no elements and has probability \(P(\emptyset)=0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    An impossible event has probability 0.
    🔗
    A sure thing (certain event) includes all outcomes in \(S\) and has probability \(P(S)=1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition H.4.844. Probability Properties.

    • For any event \(E\text{,}\) \(0\leq P(E)\leq 1\text{.}\)
      🔗
    • The total probability over a sample space is 1.
      🔗
    • If events \(E\) and \(F\) are disjoint, then \(P(E\text{ or }F)=P(E)+P(F)\text{.}\)
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition H.4.849. What are Mutually Exclusive Events?

    Two events are mutually exclusive if they cannot happen at the same time.
    🔗
    \(P(A\text{ and }B)=0\text{,}\) equivalently \(A\cap B=\emptyset\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Definition H.4.851. What is the Addition Rule for Probability?

    For any events \(E\) and \(F\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} P(E\text{ or }F)=P(E)+P(F)-P(E\text{ and }F) \end{equation*}

    If \(E\) and \(F\) are mutually exclusive:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} P(E\text{ or }F)=P(E)+P(F) \end{equation*}

    Definition H.4.876. What is the Complement of an Event?

    The complement of event \(E\text{,}\) denoted \(E^c\) or not \(E\text{,}\) consists of all outcomes in the sample space not in \(E\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} P(E)+P(\text{not }E)=1 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} P(\text{not }E)=1-P(E) \end{equation*}
