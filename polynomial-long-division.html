    Section 7.7 The Division Algorithm

    Subsection 7.7.1 Natural Numbers Version

    Theorem 7.7.1. The Division Algorithm for Natural Numbers.

    Let \(p\) and \(d\) be natural numbers (nonzero, positive, whole numbers). Then there exist two unique numbers, a quotient \(q\) and a remainder \(r\text{,}\) such that
    \begin{equation*} p = q\cdot d + r\text{,} \end{equation*}
    where \(r < d\text{.}\)
    • \(p\) is known as the dividend.
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(d\) is known as the divisor.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    The Division Algorithm gives us a way to define division in terms of multiplication and addition for any two numbers \(p\) and \(d\text{.}\) We find the values \(q\) and \(r\) using long division. Our next two examples show us what happens.
    🔗

    Example 7.7.2. Long Division with a Non-Zero Remainder.

    Use long division to divide \(37\) by \(5\text{.}\) Then express the result using the Division Algorithm.
    🔗
    Answer.
    \begin{equation*} 37 = 7(5) + 2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    First, we set up the long division as follows:
    🔗
    Then, we ask ourselves, how many times does \(5\) divide into \(37\text{?}\) It divides 7 times, and we write the \(7\) above the one’s digit of 37 and above the long division symbol as follows:
    🔗
    We then multiply \(7\) by \(5\) to get \(35\) and write that below the \(37\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Then we subtract \(35\) from \(37\) to get \(2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Since \(2 < 5\text{,}\) the Division Algorithm says we can stop dividing. The number above the long division symbol is the quotient \(q = 7\text{,}\) and the \(2\) we get after the subtraction is the remainder \(r\text{.}\) Therefore, the Division Algorithm says that
    \begin{equation*} 37 = 7(5) + 2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 7.7.7. Long Division with a Zero Remainder.

    Use long division to divide \(136\) by \(4\text{.}\) Then express the result using the Division Algorithm.
    🔗
    Answer.
    \begin{equation*} 136 = 34(4) + 0 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    First, we set up the long division as follows:
    🔗
    We ask ourselves, how many times does \(4\) divide into the first digit, \(1\text{?}\) It does not divide evenly, so we consider the first two digits, \(13\text{.}\) The number \(4\) divides into \(13\) three times, since \(3 × 4 = 12\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We multiply \(3 \cdot 4 = 12\) and write \(12\) below \(13\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Then, we subtract to get \(1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We then bring down the next digit, \(6\text{,}\) to make \(16\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The number \(4\) divides into \(16\) exactly \(4\) times, and we write the \(4\) above the one’s digit in \(136\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Since \(4\cdot 4=16\text{,}\) we write \(16\) below the existing \(16\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We subtract \(16\) from \(16\) to get \(0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Since \(r = 0\text{,}\) the division ends here. The number above the long division symbol is the quotient \(q = 34\text{,}\) and the remainder is \(r = 0\text{.}\) Therefore, the Division Algorithm says that
    \begin{equation*} 136 = 34(4) + 0\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Notice that, in our last example,
    \begin{equation*} 136 = 34(4) + 0 \end{equation*}
    simplifies to
    \begin{equation*} 136 = 34(4)\text{.} \end{equation*}
    We just proved that \(136\) factors as \(34\) and \(4\text{.}\) Sound familiar? This is what we want to do with polynomials. Fortunately, a Division Algorithm for polynomials exists as well!
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 7.7.2 Polynomial Version

    Before we state the Division Algorithm for Polynomials, we need to define what is the “zero” for polynomials.
    🔗

    Definition 7.7.16. The Zero Polynomial.

    The zero polynomial is the polynomial whose value is zero for every value of \(x\text{.}\) We write the zero polynomial as
    \begin{equation*} p(x) = 0 \end{equation*}
    for all real numbers \(x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 7.7.17.

    Don’t confuse the zero polynomial \(p(x) = 0\) with solving a polynomial equation \(p(x) = 0\text{.}\) While the notations are identical, the contexts are very different. Thankfully, these concepts don’t typically come into direct contact with one another, so the context of the problem will tell you which version of the notation we are using.
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 7.7.18. The Division Algorithm.

    Let \(p(x)\) and \(d(x)\) be polynomials, where \(d(x)\) is not the zero polynomial. Then there exist unique polynomials, a quotient \(q(x)\) and a remainder \(r(x)\text{,}\) such that
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} p(x) = q(x) d(x) + r(x) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    where the degree of \(r(x)\) is less than the degree of \(d(x)\text{.}\)
    • \(p(x)\) is called the dividend.
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(d(x)\) is called the divisor.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 7.7.19. Polynomial Long Division Example.

    Use polynomial long division to divide \(p(x) = x^2 + 3x - 1\) by \(d(x) = x + 4\text{.}\) Then express the result using the Division Algorithm for polynomials.
    🔗
    Answer.
    \begin{equation*} x^2 + 3x - 1 = (x - 1)(x + 4) + 3 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    First, we set up the division as follows.
    🔗
    We begin by writing a fraction: the numerator is the leading term of \(p(x)\) and the denominator is the leading term of \(d(x)\text{.}\) We then simplify this fraction, and this is the number of times the leading term of the divisor divides the leading term of the dividend. In this case, we get the following:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x^2}{x}=x. \end{equation*}
    Since \(x\) is a degree 1 term, we will write it above the degree 1 term of the dividend.
    🔗
    Next, we multiply the divisor \((x + 4)\) by \(x\) to get \(x^2 + 4x\text{.}\) We write this result below the dividend, make sure the terms with the same powers are vertically lined up. We then write a line under this expression.
    🔗
    Next, we subtract \(x^2 + 4x\) from the dividend \(x^2 + 3x - 1\)
    🔗
    \begin{gather*} (x^2 + 3x - 1) - (x^2 + 4x) \\\\ = -x - 1 \end{gather*}
    🔗
    and write that result below the horizontal line we drew.
    🔗
    Again, we write a fraction: the numerator is the leading term of \(-x-1\) and the denominator is the leading term of \(d(x)\text{.}\) We then simplify this fraction to get the next division:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{-x}{x}=-1. \end{equation*}
    We write the \(-1\) above the constant term of the dividend
    🔗
    and multiply \(-1\) by the divisor \(x+4\) to get \(-x-4\text{.}\) We write this product below \(-x-1\) and write a line below it.
    🔗
    Now we subtract the two expressions
    🔗
    \begin{gather*} (-x - 1) - (-x - 4) \\\\ = 3 \end{gather*}
    🔗
    and write the 3 below the horizontal line we drew.
    🔗
    Since the remainder \(r(x) = 3\) has degree 0 and the divisor \(d(x) = x + 4\) has degree 1, the Division Algorithm tells us we can stop here. The quotient is \(q(x) = x - 1\) and the remainder is \(r(x) = 3\text{.}\) Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x^2 + 3x - 1 = (x - 1)(x + 4) + 3 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 7.7.27. Polynomial Long Division Example.

    Use polynomial long division to divide \(p(x) = x^4 - x^2 - 2x - 1\) by \(d(x) = x^2 - x - 1\text{.}\) Then express the result using the Division Algorithm for polynomials.
    🔗
    Answer.
    \begin{equation*} x^4 - x^2 - 2x - 1 = (x^2 + x + 1)(x^2 - x - 1) + 0 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    First, we set up the division as follows.
    🔗
    We begin by writing a fraction: the numerator is the leading term of \(p(x)\) and the denominator is the leading term of \(d(x)\text{.}\) We then simplify this fraction; the result tells us how many times the leading term of the divisor divides the leading term of the dividend. In this case,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x^4}{x^2} = x^2. \end{equation*}
    Since \(x^2\) is degree 2, we write it above the degree 2 term of the dividend.
    🔗
    Next, we multiply the divisor \((x^2 - x - 1)\) by \(x^2\) to get \(x^4 - x^3 - x^2\text{.}\) We write this result below the dividend, aligning like powers, and draw a horizontal line.
    🔗
    Now subtract:
    🔗
    \begin{gather*} (x^4 + 0x^3 - x^2 - 2x - 1) - (x^4 - x^3 - x^2) \\\\ = x^3 - 2x - 1 \end{gather*}
    🔗
    Again, form the fraction of leading terms and simplify to get the next quotient term:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x^3}{x^2} = x. \end{equation*}
    We write \(x\) in the quotient.
    🔗
    Multiply \(x\) by the divisor \(x^2 - x - 1\) to get \(x^3 - x^2 - x\text{.}\) Write this below \(x^3 - 2x - 1\) and draw a line.
    🔗
    Subtract again:
    🔗
    \begin{gather*} (x^3 - 2x - 1) - (x^3 - x^2 - x) \\\\ = x^2 - x - 1 \end{gather*}
    🔗
    Form the leading-term fraction once more:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x^2}{x^2} = 1. \end{equation*}
    We write \(1\) in the quotient.
    🔗
    Multiply \(1\) by the divisor to get \(x^2 - x - 1\text{,}\) write it below, and subtract:
    🔗
    \begin{gather*} (x^2 - x - 1) - (x^2 - x - 1) \\\\ = 0 \end{gather*}
    🔗
    Since the remainder \(r(x) = 0\) has degree less than the divisor \(d(x) = x^2 - x - 1\text{,}\) the Division Algorithm tells us we can stop here. The quotient is \(q(x) = x^2 + x + 1\) and the remainder is \(r(x) = 0\text{.}\) Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x^4 - x^2 - 2x - 1 = (x^2 + x + 1)(x^2 - x - 1) + 0 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Just like with integers, notice that when the remainder is zero, we get a factorization of the polynomials. Our next goal is to find a consistent way to divide polynomials to get remainders of zero. But first, we will introduce a simpler way to divide polynomials of certain forms in the next section.
