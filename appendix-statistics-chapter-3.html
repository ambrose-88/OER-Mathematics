    Definition H.3.524. Mean.

    The mean, also called the average, is the sum of all data values divided by the number of values.
    🔗
    Population Mean: \(\mu=\dfrac{\sum x}{N}\)
    🔗
    Sample Mean: \(\overline{x}=\dfrac{\sum x}{n}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition H.3.525. What do these symbols mean?

    • \(\sum\) means sum or add all values.
      🔗
    • \(x\) represents individual data values.
      🔗
    • \(N\) is population size.
      🔗
    • \(n\) is sample size.
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition H.3.526. Why do we have two formulas for mean?

    We use one formula for populations and one for samples because they represent different contexts and notations.
    🔗
    🔗

    🔗

    Definition H.3.531. Median.

    The median of an ordered dataset separates the lower 50% and upper 50%. It may or may not be a data value and is often denoted \(M\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition H.3.532. How do I calculate the Median?

    Let \(n\) be sample size.
    🔗
    • Order data from smallest to largest.
      🔗
    • Check parity of \(n\text{:}\)
      • If \(n\) is odd, median is entry \(\dfrac{n+1}{2}\text{.}\)
        🔗
      • If \(n\) is even, median is average of entries \(\dfrac{n}{2}\) and \(\dfrac{n}{2}+1\text{.}\)
        🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition H.3.538. Mode.

    The mode of a dataset is the value(s) that occur locally with highest frequency.
    🔗
    When discussing the shape of histograms, we use the following classifications:
    🔗
    • A unimodal distribution has one peak.
      🔗
      🔗
    • A multimodal distribution has multiple peaks.
      🔗
      🔗
    • A uniform distribution has no distinct peak.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition H.3.542. Revised Definition: Mode.

    The mode is the value(s) that occur most frequently.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition H.3.547. Outliers.

    An outlier is a data point that does not follow the overall distribution pattern and is usually much larger or smaller than most values.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example H.3.548. Example 1.

    Given scores \(78,\ 82,\ 84,\ 91,\ 91\text{:}\)

      🔗
    • Add outlier 41 and recompute.
      🔗
    • Determine which center changed most and least.
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition H.3.549. Resistance to Outliers.

    A statistic is resistant if extreme values cause little or no change.
    🔗
    In this context, mean is not resistant, while median and mode are resistant.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition H.3.550. The Mean, Median, and Mode in Unimodal Distributions.

    Normal Distribution: \(\text{mean}=\text{median}=\text{mode}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Skew-right Distribution: \(\text{mode}<\text{median}<\text{mean}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Skew-left Distribution: \(\text{mean}<\text{median}<\text{mode}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition H.3.555. What is a Weighted Mean?

    A weighted mean is a mean where values carry frequencies or weights.
    🔗
    Using frequencies: \(\overline{x}=\dfrac{\sum(f\cdot x)}{\sum f}\)
    🔗
    Using weights: \(\overline{x}=\dfrac{\sum(w\cdot x)}{\sum w}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition H.3.560. Is the Mean Enough to Describe a Dataset?

    The mean is one of the most commonly used measures to summarize a dataset. While it provides useful information about central tendency, it does not always tell the whole story. Different datasets can have the same mean and still have very different distributions.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition H.3.568. Deviation.

    The deviation of a data point \(x\) is the difference between the value and the mean.
    🔗
    Population: \(\text{deviation}=x-\mu\)
    🔗
    Sample: \(\text{deviation}=x-\overline{x}\)
    🔗
    \(\text{deviation}=\text{data}-\text{mean}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition H.3.569. Notes on Deviation.

    • Positive deviation means the data point is above the mean.
      🔗
    • Negative deviation means the data point is below the mean.
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition H.3.572. Understanding Standard Deviation.

    Standard deviation is a key measure of variability and describes typical distance from the mean.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition H.3.573. Squared Deviation.

    Population: \((x-\mu)^2\)
    🔗
    Sample: \((x-\overline{x})^2\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example H.3.574. Example 1.

    Compute squared deviations for the salary data and find their sum.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition H.3.575. Variance.

    Population: \(\sigma^2=\dfrac{\sum (x-\mu)^2}{N}\)
    🔗
    Sample: \(s^2=\dfrac{\sum (x-\overline{x})^2}{n-1}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition H.3.576. Why does sample variance use \(n-1\text{?}\)

    This is related to degrees of freedom.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example H.3.580. Example 2.

    Compute variance for the salary dataset.
    🔗
    • Variance has squared units.
      🔗
    • Variance can be numerically large because deviations are squared.
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition H.3.581. Standard Deviation.

    Population: \(\sigma=\sqrt{\dfrac{\sum (x-\mu)^2}{N}}\)
    🔗
    Sample: \(s=\sqrt{\dfrac{\sum (x-\overline{x})^2}{n-1}}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition H.3.582. Important Properties of Standard Deviation.

    • Measures average distance from the mean.
      🔗
    • Always greater than or equal to zero.
      🔗
    • Larger values indicate more variation.
      🔗
    • Not resistant to outliers.
      🔗
    • Uses the same units as the data.
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example H.3.583. Example 3.

    Use the Summary Statistics Calculator to compute standard deviation for the salary data.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition H.3.584. How to Find Outliers for Normal Distributions.

    Outliers are extreme values that stand apart from most of a dataset.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition H.3.586. The Empirical Rule.

    • About \(68\%\) of data lies between \(\mu-\sigma\) and \(\mu+\sigma\text{.}\)
      🔗
    • About \(95\%\) of data lies between \(\mu-2\sigma\) and \(\mu+2\sigma\text{.}\)
      🔗
    • About \(99.7\%\) of data lies between \(\mu-3\sigma\) and \(\mu+3\sigma\text{.}\)
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

      🔗

      🔗
    🔗

    Definition H.3.588. Chebyshev’s Inequality.

    At least \(\left(1-\dfrac{1}{k^2}\right)\times 100\%\) of data lies within \(k\) standard deviations of the mean, for \(k>1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    • \(k=2\text{:}\) at least \(75\%\)
      🔗
    • \(k=3\text{:}\) at least \(88.9\%\)
      🔗
    • \(k=4\text{:}\) at least \(93.75\%\)
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example H.3.589. Example 1.

    Definition H.3.590. How to Calculate a Percentile.

    A percentile indicates the relative standing of a data value in a dataset. The \(p^{\text{th}}\) percentile is the value below which \(p\%\) of the data falls.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition H.3.591. Percentiles By Hand.

    Formula for percentile rank of a value \(x\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \(\text{Percentile}=\left(\dfrac{\text{number of values less than }x}{\text{total number of values}}\right)\times 100\)
    🔗
    How to find a data value for a given percentile:
    🔗
    • Step 1: Compute \(\dfrac{p}{100}\times n\text{.}\)
      🔗
    • Step 2: Use the position in the ordered data:
      • If decimal, use adjacent positions and average those two values.
        🔗
      • If whole number, use that position and the next position, then average.
        🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Notation: \(P_n\) denotes the \(n^{\text{th}}\) percentile (for example, \(P_{45}\)).
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition H.3.598. How to Calculate a \(z\)-score.

    A \(z\)-score (standard score) measures how many standard deviations a value lies above or below the mean.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example H.3.599. Example 1.

    A normal distribution has mean 175 cm and standard deviation 7 cm.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition H.3.601. \(z\)-Score Formulas.

    General structure: \(z=\dfrac{\text{data}-\text{mean}}{\text{standard deviation}}\)
    🔗
    Population: \(z=\dfrac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\)
    🔗
    Sample: \(z=\dfrac{x-\overline{x}}{s}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition H.3.603. Important Features of \(z\)-Scores.

    • \(z\)-scores are unitless.
      🔗
    • Positive means above the mean; negative means below.
      🔗
    • Absolute \(z\)-score measures extremeness.
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example H.3.605. Example 2.

    Who is relatively taller at 68 inches: a man or a woman?
    🔗
    🔗

    Example H.3.607. Example 3.

    The ACT has mean 20.8 and standard deviation 5.8. The SAT has mean 1500 and standard deviation 300. Who performed worse relative to peers: SAT 1075 or ACT 15?
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition H.3.608. Extreme Values.

    A value is more extreme when its absolute \(z\)-score is larger.
    🔗
    • Step 1: Compute both \(z\)-scores.
      🔗
    • Step 2: Take absolute values.
      🔗
    • Step 3: Larger absolute value is more extreme.
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition H.3.618. How to Find Outliers for Skewed Data.

    The interquartile range is \(\text{IQR}=Q_3-Q_1\text{,}\) and it measures spread of the middle 50% of skewed data.
    🔗
    🔗

      🔗
    • Significantly high if value \(>Q_3+1.5(\text{IQR})\text{.}\)

    Definition H.3.624. How to Create a Boxplot.

    A boxplot summarizes the distribution using the five-number summary and whiskers.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition H.3.625. Steps to Construct a Boxplot.

    1. Find the five-number summary.
      🔗
    2. Draw a box from \(Q_1\) to \(Q_3\) with a median line.
      🔗
    3. Draw whiskers to minimum and maximum.
      🔗
    🔗

    Definition H.3.630. Skewness in Boxplots.

    • Approximately normal: median centered and whiskers similar length.
      🔗
    • Left skewed: median closer to \(Q_3\) or left whisker longer.
      🔗
    • Right skewed: median closer to \(Q_1\) or right whisker longer.
